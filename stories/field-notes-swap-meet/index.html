Field notes from a swap meet

Video Communities Revisited

Notes from a morning among folding tables, a thousand tapes and one very patient man with a splicing machine.

We spent a Saturday morning at a monthly swap meet in a community hall: six trestle tables, a coffee urn, and a sound system playing the same eight bars of something all day. About two hundred people came through, and nearly everyone was carrying something — a shoebox of Betamax tapes, a splicing machine, a player with a chewed-up tape door.

Three things we did not expect

Trading is not about the value of the tape. Most of the exchanges we watched were low-value recordings — home movies, holiday tapes, copies of copies — being traded up for something more interesting. The tape was the excuse; the point was the conversation about where the tape had come from.

Repair is a social skill. The man with the splicing machine was the most popular person at the hall, and not because he fixed anything especially well. He was popular because he was willing to look at a broken tape with you. Several people asked him about machines they had given up on. He fixed none of them, but three people went home and tried their tapes again.

Cataloguing is memory work. A stallholder had written a note in biro on the back of a cardboard box: “B’s tapes, box 2, don’t pay D, he’s annoying.” Nobody could say who B or D were. The note stayed on the box.

What this means for the project

Swap meets are not a relic of the VHS era; they are the clearest example we have of how physical media keeps communities going. They are also nearly invisible in the archives, because a swap meet leaves no institutional trace at all. That is why we are relying on the people who were there.
