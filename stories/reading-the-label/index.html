14 August 2026 sample

Reading the label

Video Communities Revisited

A tape is an object before it is a recording. What the label, the case and the handwriting can tell us.

Before we watch anything, we look at the thing itself. The label on a VHS tape is a small piece of writing that tells you more than it was designed to: who rented it, from where, when, and what they thought of it.

A rental sticker from a shop in a town you have never visited carries the shop’s name, a phone number, a weekly rate, and a date. A second sticker from a different shop, in a different hand, tells you the tape travelled. A hand-written title on masking tape tells you somebody cared enough to write it, and usually what they thought the film was about: the one with the ship, rent from Mr Ali, DO NOT ERASE.

None of this is in the recording. All of it is in the object.

Why it matters for the archive

Digital workflows usually describe a tape’s contents and stop there. The label is a document in its own right, and one that survives when the recording does not. It is also a document that cataloguers routinely discard when they shoot the tape without photographing the shell.

So: we photograph the object, we transcribe what is written on it, and we treat the handwriting as data. In the long run this is what makes a collection searchable by things like “which shops in the North West used this particular sticker”, which turns out to be a surprisingly good way of reconstructing the geography of a rental market.

If you have tapes with writing on them — and most people do — we would like to see them.

Video Communities Revisited

Video Communities Revisited is a research project on the role of physical screen media — VHS, Betamax, DVD, rental shops, collectors, swap meets and boutique labels — in forming communities in Britain, past and present.
