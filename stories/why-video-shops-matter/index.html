Why video shops matter

Video Communities Revisited

A short argument for taking the rental shop seriously: not as nostalgia, but as a piece of social infrastructure.

Every so often the story of video culture is told as a story of formats: VHS beats Betamax, DVD kills VHS, streaming kills DVD. The formats are real and they matter, but they are also the least interesting part of the story.

What is more interesting is what a rental shop was. A shop on the high street was a piece of social infrastructure with an unusual set of properties. It was a place where you could ask a question and get an answer from someone who had watched the thing you were about to rent. It was a place where the staff built a mental model of a neighbourhood — who wanted westerns, who was waiting for the new Bond, who needed a copy of The Lost Weekend for a essay. It was a place where the stock itself was an argument about what mattered.

None of that is captured by the disc itself. It is captured by the people.

What we are collecting

For the next three years we are collecting memories of shops, clubs and swap meets: from owners, staff and customers. We are interested in the unglamorous specifics — opening hours, the argument over the late fees, the customers who had a standing order, the week the shop stopped stocking certain films.

If you worked in a video shop, or you were a regular in one, or you are still running one, we would like to hear from you. The get involved page explains what happens to what you send.

Video Communities Revisited

Video Communities Revisited is a research project on the role of physical screen media — VHS, Betamax, DVD, rental shops, collectors, swap meets and boutique labels — in forming communities in Britain, past and present.
