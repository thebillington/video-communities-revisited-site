The project

The project

What we are doing, why it matters, and who is paying for it

The short version

Video Communities Revisited is a research project about physical screen media in Britain: VHS and Betamax, DVD and Blu-ray, rental shops and swap meets, collectors and fan networks, and the small labels still pressing discs today. Streaming is often described as the end of all that. We want to know what it actually did, and what is still happening.

The project runs from 2026 to 2029 and brings together archive research, oral history and community contribution. Alongside publications and talks, we are building a public map of shops, clubs, markets and collections, and an open dataset that anyone can reuse.

Project synopsis

VCR explores changes to the social (including wellbeing), cultural, environmental and economic value of physical media, that is, analogue or digital copies of film, TV or other video material, since 2007, usually acknowledged as the beginning of the digital ‘streaming’ era. We assess the meaning and value of physical media in an age where media audiences are promised seemingly unlimited access to content via streaming, but that comes with restrictions on content availability (particularly for non-mainstream film, TV and video), as well as significant economic and (hidden) environmental costs.

VCR focuses on collectors of physical material related to film and television, and the uses, gratifications, motivations, benefits and limitations of media collecting as a pastime. Though collecting is superficially an individual, acquisitive and potentially wasteful mode of consumption aligned with the conventional needs of capitalist societies, the project experiments with alternative models of media collection: community-based, shared and sustainable.

Research questions

Our research questions include:

  1. RQ1. What are the mental health and personal wellbeing impacts of engaging in an acquisition-based hobby such as the collection of physical media assets?
  2. RQ2. What are the social possibilities afforded by physical media exchange in social spaces?
  3. RQ3. How is the contemporary economy of physical media organised, and where does it fit into the media ‘value chain’?
  4. RQ4. What are the environmental impacts of physical media, and how do these compare with those of digital media distribution?
  5. RQ5. How can the environmental waste associated with obsolete media such as DVD be avoided through the communal preservation efforts of collectors?

Method

We take a mixed methods approach that will include participant observation, questionnaire, interview, focus group and industry analysis.

Histories, legacies, futures

Histories. Video culture is usually told as a national story of boom and bust. We are more interested in the local and the ordinary: the shop on the parade, the school-hall swap meet, the club that screened things nobody else would. Trade directories, local newspapers and the memories of the people who worked in these places can together reconstruct them.

Legacies. When a shop closes, its stock scatters. Some of it becomes a collection, some of it is skipped, some of it ends up in a library or a charity shop. Legacies are about what is kept, who keeps it, and the informal archives that no institution ever collected.

Futures. Physical media has not stopped. Boutique labels make limited editions for a committed audience, restorers rebuild transfers from the best surviving tape, and community archives digitise collections that would otherwise decay. Futures asks what makes that work sustainable — equipment, skills, rights and money.

How the project works

We work regionally, with partners in archives, museums, libraries and community groups. Some work is archive-based; a great deal of it depends on people telling us what they remember and showing us what they kept. If that is you, start with Get involved.

Ethical considerations

The main ethical considerations concern the participation of non-academic partners and members of the public in our data collection phase.

Non-academic partners

We will engage partners from the media heritage sector in the project. These will include formal institutions such as libraries, archives and museums, and also less formal partners such as owners/operators of small scale video ‘museums’ which are simultaneously commercial businesses. The main risks in relation to these partnerships are reputational damage (for both partners and universities), small-scale financial loss for commercial businesses, and potential conflicts of interest. These risks are medium severity, low likelihood. We will mitigate these through open communication and dialogue with partners, who will be co-producers of research. We will also adopt written and signed participation agreements, alongside consent forms.

Members of the public

Members of the public (over 18 years old) will be engaged with for data collection, in the form of questionnaire (anonymous, though with an option to progress to interview/focus group), interview (pseudonymised), and focus group (pseudonymised). Recruitment will take place at project-related events, online and via snowballing.

We will record and transcribe interviews, which may take place face to face (to be held on university property or in public place) or via videoconferencing. Focus groups will be video recorded. We will produce Participant Information Sheets and Consent Forms to [University / department]’s template.

Participant observation will take place both at project-organised events and at public events. In the former, the presence of researchers will be advertised in advance to members of the public and will be prominently displayed at the events. For the latter, observational studies will only take place at public events in public spaces where individuals will not be identified.

Data management

We will collect questionnaire data via paper and online forms, ensuring that data management complies with the standards of [University / department] and our partner universities. We will use our institutional questionnaire consent statement.

Data from questionnaire, interview and focus groups will be stored in password protected folders on OneDrive. These will be shared only among the project team. Identifying information will not be included in these files. Contact information for participants and any other identifying information will be stored in password protected files and will only be accessed by the project team. The data will be disposed of within two years of the completion of the research.

Risks and mitigation

The main risks involved in public participation are psychological discomfort and data management. There are also risks that our sample of participants do not meet diversity, equality and inclusion standards. While the core topics for the project (physical media collecting) are non-sensitive, we will survey participants about topics related to wellbeing and social inclusion that could be sensitive.

To mitigate these risks:

  1. We will use the Demographic Information Template to gather appropriate data to ensure compliance with [University / department]’s principles of EDI.
  2. Fully brief participants by using the questionnaire consent statement, Participant Information Sheets and Consent forms.
  3. Anonymise questionnaires by only collecting relevant demographic information and including invitation to interview / focus group as a separate page. Questionnaire data will be identified only by number.
  4. Pseudonymise participants in interviews and focus groups in any published work from the project. Pseudonymised participants will be unidentifiable: they will be identified by numeral and demographic data (e.g. Participant 12 [25yo, F, Nottingham, Black]). Documents to identify participants (to enable withdrawal if requested) will be password protected.
  5. Data management will comply with [University / department] principles.
  6. The participant information sheet will explain that participants in interview/focus group are free to withdraw from the project at any time. Their data will be removed from project files from the point of withdrawal.
  7. Potential psychological discomfort will be mitigated via content notices in participant information sheets / questionnaire consent statement.
