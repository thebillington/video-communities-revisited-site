The project

The project

What we are doing, why it matters, and who is paying for it

The short version

Video Communities Revisited is a research project about physical screen media in Britain: VHS and Betamax, DVD and Blu-ray, rental shops and swap meets, collectors and fan networks, and the small labels still pressing discs today. Streaming is often described as the end of all that. We want to know what it actually did, and what is still happening.

The project runs from 2026 to 2029 and brings together archive research, oral history and community contribution. Alongside publications and talks, we are building a public map of shops, clubs, markets and collections, and an open dataset that anyone can reuse.

Research questions

Our work starts from four questions:

  1. How do physical media formats shape the social life of film and television — who gathers around a tape, a shelf, a stall?
  2. What did rental shops, video clubs and swap meets do for their neighbourhoods, and what survives of them?
  3. How do collectors, archivists and enthusiasts keep formats usable today, and what equipment, skills and networks does that depend on?
  4. What futures are being built for physical media, from boutique labels and restoration to community archiving?

Histories, legacies, futures

Histories. Video culture is usually told as a national story of boom and bust. We are more interested in the local and the ordinary: the shop on the parade, the school-hall swap meet, the club that screened things nobody else would. Trade directories, local newspapers and the memories of the people who worked in these places can together reconstruct them.

Legacies. When a shop closes, its stock scatters. Some of it becomes a collection, some of it is skipped, some of it ends up in a library or a charity shop. Legacies are about what is kept, who keeps it, and the informal archives that no institution ever collected.

Futures. Physical media has not stopped. Boutique labels make limited editions for a committed audience, restorers rebuild transfers from the best surviving tape, and community archives digitise collections that would otherwise decay. Futures asks what makes that work sustainable — equipment, skills, rights and money.

How the project works

We work regionally, with partners in archives, museums, libraries and community groups. Some work is archive-based; a great deal of it depends on people telling us what they remember and showing us what they kept. If that is you, start with Get involved.
